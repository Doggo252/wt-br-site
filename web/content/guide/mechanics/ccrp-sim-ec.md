Air Simulator Enduring Confrontation (Sim EC) has no markers, so nothing on screen tells you where the Mission Targets and bases are or which one you've picked. Finding a target and actually hitting it is half the job, and CCRP (Continuously Calculated Release Point) bombing computers are the best tool for it.

Every CCRP method works the same way at heart: you mark or select a target, fly towards it, and the bombing computer decides the moment to release so the bombs land on it. What changes from aircraft to aircraft is how you mark the target and how much the sight tells you. This page goes from the oldest systems to the newest.

## First: set up weapon selection

There are two ways to drop bombs, and players coming from other modes often mix them up.

- **Drop bomb / Drop bomb series.** The simple keys. "Drop bomb" releases one bomb or a pair, depending on the plane. "Drop bomb series" releases the number you set under Bomb series on the briefing screen. The problem is that you can't choose which bomb type goes if you carry several, and you can't change the series size in flight. Not recommended for Sim.
- **Weapon selection (recommended).** Use "Switch secondary weapon" to cycle through what you carry (bombs, torpedoes, rockets, missiles and so on) and "Fire secondary weapon" to release it. One fire key does everything, and you choose exactly which bomb you drop.

With weapon selection, "Secondary weapon ripple quantity" sets how many go at once: single by default, then 2, 4, 8, 16 and so on, and finally S for series, which keeps releasing that bomb type until you let go of the key or run out.

Those steps don't always fit your load. At high tier a base takes, for example, six Mk.83 1000-pound bombs. A Tornado can carry 12, enough for two bases, but there's no ripple setting of 6. One workaround is to carry six low-drag and six high-drag bombs, so each group of six can go as its own series.

## 1. Early bombing computers

The oldest systems are simple: aim at the target with your sight, switch the computer on and pull up. From your altitude, speed, rate of descent, the bomb's ballistics and some settings the ground crew enter before the sortie, the computer works out the release point and drops the bombs itself when you reach it. The Saab BT ("Bomb Tosser") family is a well-known example, fitted in the game to aircraft such as the Étendard IVM, P-16 and Hunter Mk.58.

How to do it:

1. Come in on a descent with your wings level.
2. Put your sight on the point you want to hit.
3. Mark it with the "Activate target point" key.
4. Press and hold the bomb (pickle) button.
5. Pull back smoothly and steadily to level off or climb, keeping your heading and bank steady.
6. The bombs release on their own when the computer decides it's time.
7. Break away.

The big drawback: once you've marked a point, the sight doesn't show where it is, so you can't check what you marked.

The upside is that this method works on anything, not just Mission Targets: ground vehicles, bunkers and so on. That makes it very handy in ground battle events with aircraft that lack CCIP (Continuously Calculated Impact Point). Every CCRP-equipped aircraft can use it, not only the early ones.

## 2. Computers with pre-programmed targets

Later computers, as in the Milan, know the Mission Targets and bases in advance. The sight is still basic, but the ring of the gyro sight moves to show the heading you need to fly to pass over the selected target. It gives no altitude, no distance and no countdown to release.

How to do it:

1. Line up roughly on your target, close enough to straight that it falls inside the sight's field of view. You can do this from very far out; the map helps.
2. Cycle through the Mission Targets until the sight's ring sits over the one you want.
3. Fly in, line up so you'll pass directly over it, and once you're close enough press and hold the bomb button.
4. The computer releases the bombs at the right point.
5. Break away.

## 3. Modern computers with a detailed HUD

The next step adds more information: besides the direction, the HUD shows where the target is on the ground (which tells you its altitude) and a countdown to release. That makes planning a run much easier. Aircraft such as the Buccaneers, A-7s, Harriers, Jaguars, Su-22s and Su-25s have this kind of sight, among many others.

The technique is the same as in section 2; you simply have more information to work with.

## 4. Computers linked to targeting equipment

The last stage connects the bombing computer to a targeting sensor, either built in (for example on the A-6E TRAM or F-117) or carried as a targeting pod, as on many modern aircraft.

It works like section 3, with two extras. After selecting a Mission Target you can switch to the targeting view and place the aim point precisely where you want. And you can aim at any point target, such as vehicles or bunkers, which again makes it very useful in ground battle events.

## 5. Toss bombing (lofting)

CCRP computers also let you toss bombs. Instead of releasing in level flight or a dive, you release while climbing, so the bombs fly up and over in an arc. You can drop from much further out and stay outside the air defences around a heavily protected target.

How to do it:

1. Mark or select the target using any of the methods above.
2. From level flight or a dive, press and hold the bomb button.
3. Pull back smoothly and steadily into a climb, keeping heading and bank steady.
4. The computer releases the bombs when it has the right point.
5. Break away.

The hard part is judging when to pull up and how steep a climb to hold. That depends a lot on your starting altitude, speed and range, and takes some practice. A HUD with a release countdown helps enormously, but lofting works with every method on this page.

Don't bother lofting high-drag bombs: their retarders cancel out the benefit of the upward arc.

## 6. Special cases

### Mission Targets with bugged aim points

On some maps the pre-set aim point of certain Mission Targets sits at the wrong height, so the bombs aren't released when the countdown runs out. It's most often seen on Afghanistan, Vietnam and some bases on Denmark, but it can happen on any map. Two ways round it:

- Don't cycle through the pre-programmed targets. Mark the target with your sight instead and attack as in section 1.
- If you have targeting equipment, select the target, switch to the targeting view and move the aim point so it sits exactly on the target.

### Aircraft with CCRP but no cockpit cues

Quite a few aircraft that had CCRP-capable bombing computers in real life currently show no CCRP indications in the cockpit. In Arcade and Realistic this hardly matters, because HUD-only and external views add CCRP overlays. In Sim, though, you can select a Mission Target but have no way of knowing which one you selected, or how to fly to it. Affected aircraft include, among others:

- F-111 Aardvark
- F-105 Thunderchief
- F-4 Phantom II
- A-4 Skyhawk

In these aircraft, use the section 1 method, or CCIP if the aircraft has it.

### Marking targets on the tactical map

The "Storm Warning" update added useful tools to the tactical map: you can pan it, zoom in (for example with the mouse wheel) and place a marker, a red square bracket, anywhere on it.

That marker becomes the point of interest (POI, also called SPI, single point of interest) for your ballistic computer, which makes it the aim point for every CCRP method above. Whatever your aircraft has, such as weapon seekers, targeting equipment and CCRP marks on the sight or HUD, will point at it. So instead of marking with the sight or cycling through targets, you can pick the target straight off the map.

How to do it:

1. Open the tactical map.
2. Find your target, zooming with the mouse wheel and panning by holding the right mouse button and dragging as needed.
3. Click the middle mouse button as precisely as you can on the target to drop the marker.
4. Close the map.
5. Set up your sight or HUD, weapons and delivery method.
6. Follow the steering and aiming cues to the target.
7. If the marker isn't quite in the right place, adjust the aim point with the methods described earlier.

Some cautions:

- Zoom in as far as you can before placing the marker. Placed accurately, it's good enough on its own for area targets such as Mission Targets and bases, especially with area weapons like incendiary bombs.
- Marking a target any other way (with the sight or HUD, by cycling bases, by aiming a seeker or the targeting equipment) replaces the map marker. To go back to the map point, place it again.
- To move the marker, go through the steps again and take care when placing it. It's still a bit fiddly: if the marker tool isn't selected, the worst outcome is that you mark the spot for your team instead, which is useful anyway since it shows them what you're attacking.
- Fiddling with the map in flight is dangerous. The ground gets closer faster than you'd think.
