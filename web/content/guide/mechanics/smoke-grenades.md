A smoke grenade launcher lets a vehicle throw up a screen in a few seconds. Not all smoke is the same: the types in the game differ in how quickly they work and in how they are delivered. Some are fired from a launcher as small shells, others are canisters dropped onto the ground. Launchers can be mounted outside on the armour or built into the fighting compartment.

## The basics

- **Limited supply.** Every vehicle with smoke carries a fixed number of charges.
- **Single shots or salvos.** External banks, for example on the back of a turret, may fire one charge or several per press. Internal launchers in the turret, as on the Churchill III, fire one round at a time until they run out.
- **Short-lived clouds.** Each charge makes smoke when it lands or bursts, and the cloud only lasts so long.
- **They can be shot.** Smoke launchers are damageable modules, and grenades hit by a shell go off and release their smoke where they are.
- **Controls.** The default key is G. To change it, go to Controls → Ground Vehicles → Weaponry → Smoke Grenades.

## The three types

| Type | Delivery | Time to deploy | Duration | Example vehicles |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary smoke grenades | Small canisters or shells that make smoke where they land | About 5 s | 20 s | Pz.IV G, Churchill III, SAV 20.12.48 |
| Modern smoke grenades | Fired to put smoke directly in front of the vehicle, over a larger area | About 2 s | 20 s | JPz 4-5, M1A2 Abrams, T-80U |
| Large smoke bombs (BDSh) | Released from the rear, making smoke directly behind the vehicle | About 5 s | 20 s | T-44-100, T-54 (1947), T-10M |

### Ordinary smoke grenades

The kind most often found on Second World War tanks. Launchers sit either inside the turret, for single, aimed shots, or in external banks on the turret sides, which usually fire short salvos.

### Modern smoke grenades

An upgraded charge found on today's main battle tanks. Besides deploying much faster, it makes your spotting marker vanish sooner, which is often what saves you.

### Large smoke bombs

Rear-mounted canisters that are dropped rather than fired, leaving a screen behind the tank. Several Soviet and post-Soviet vehicles carry them, which makes them handy for covering a retreat.

For a bigger screen you can lay on the move, see the [engine smoke system](/guide/mechanics/engine-smoke-system/).

## A short history

Smoke was one of the earliest and simplest ways to keep tanks alive, and smoke devices appeared almost as soon as tanks went into mass service. British and German vehicles had them from the early 1930s: early Pz.IV models carried several drop-type canisters at the rear, and some British tanks used improvised launchers made from Lee-Enfield rifles.

By the middle of the war, factory-made turret mortars had become the usual mounting. External launchers were easily damaged by shrapnel and sometimes forced the crew to climb out to reload them, so some designs moved them inside the hull or turret bustle. Germany used smoke launchers widely, while British and American crews more often fired smoke rounds from the main gun.

Soviet tanks got smoke devices in the middle of the war, first MDSh naval smoke canisters and then BDSh canisters designed for armoured vehicles. Released from the rear, they were fitted to the T-34-85, T-44 and SU-100 among others, and stayed in use into the 1950s until thermo-smoke systems replaced them.

Modern smoke grenades can hide a vehicle within seconds, and charges mixed with metallic flakes or similar particles can also weaken infrared-seeking missiles and thermal imagers by masking the vehicle's heat signature.
