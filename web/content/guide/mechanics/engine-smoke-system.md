An engine smoke system makes a smoke screen out of the vehicle's own fuel. Fuel is sprayed onto very hot engine parts, such as the exhaust manifold or turbine blades, where it flashes into vapour. As that vapour hits the cooler air it condenses into a thick white cloud. Most vehicles with ESS are Cold War or modern ground vehicles, and some ships and floatplanes can lay smoke too.

## What it does in battle

- **A large cloud around you.** Activating ESS fills the area near your vehicle with smoke. Every vehicle has a limited number of uses.
- **Hides you and your teammates.** Anything inside the cloud is hidden from view, and spotting markers on vehicles in it disappear.
- **Does not stop thermals.** ESS smoke is cooler than the smoke from grenades, so it cannot blind thermal imagers. An enemy with a thermal sight can see straight through it.
- **Draws attention.** A big plume of smoke tells everyone where you are. Use it deliberately, and remember that [smoke grenades](/guide/mechanics/smoke-grenades/) or smoke [shells](/shells/) may be the better choice in some situations.

## Good ways to use it

- **Screening a capture point.** Drive laps round the point in a fast vehicle with ESS running. The resulting wall of smoke blocks the enemy's view of the point and slows their push.
- **Covering a retreat or a repair.** A quick screen gives a damaged teammate a few moments to pull back or fix their vehicle.
- **Fighting tanks without thermals.** A vehicle that has both ESS and a thermal sight, such as the Sho't Kal Dalet, can hide in its own smoke and still see out of it. It is situational, but against older tanks without thermals it can win you the exchange.

## How the real system works

ESS runs on thermal condensation: the hot engine evaporates the fuel and the atmosphere turns the vapour into fog. The amount of fuel decides what it can hide. Screening the visible spectrum (0.4–0.76 μm) typically uses about 0.17 kg of diesel per second; raising that to 1.7 kg/s extends the screen into the near and middle infrared (0.4–3.0 μm). In real life, then, a heavy enough screen can work against thermal imagers, even though in the game it does not.

It only works with a warm engine. Crews avoid changing gear while laying smoke, because sudden changes in engine speed break up the screen. At low load or idle the exhaust is not hot enough to evaporate all the fuel, so some escapes unvapourised and the screen comes out thin.

## A short history

- **Second World War.** The Soviet T-34 and KV-1 could carry the MDSh, a 40 kg naval smoke canister that made a cloud 500 to 600 metres long lasting about 9 minutes. Its mountings could take ordinary diesel tanks instead when no canisters were available.
- **Cold War.** Fuel-vapour ESS proper came later. The 1973 Yom Kippur War showed that smoke screens cut losses to anti-tank guided missiles, which pushed development of a system to back up smoke grenades. The Soviets fitted ESS from the T-55 in the 1950s and kept it on most later tanks, including the T-72.
- **United States.** The US Army developed the Vehicle Engine Exhaust Smoke System (VEESS), letting tanks lay smoke on the move; it was used on the M60 and M1 Abrams. An exhaust-based system for the M60A1 RISE, made in the UK for the Edgewood Arsenal, passed trials in August 1975. VEESS uses diesel as its smoke agent, so its duration depends on the fuel supply, and it works together with the tank's grenade launchers.

Today the Russian Army also uses dedicated smoke-generator vehicles such as the TDA-3 to lay large screens quickly.
