An anti-tank guided missile (ATGM) is a missile designed to destroy heavily armoured vehicles such as tanks. What separates it from a rocket is guidance: a rocket follows its ballistic path once it leaves the launcher, while a missile can be steered all the way to the target, either by hand or by keeping a sight on it.

ATGMs arrived in Update 1.59 "Flaming Arrows", which gave every nation a missile-armed vehicle, and many more have been added since. You can look up missiles alongside other rounds in our [ammunition browser](/shells/).

## Guidance by game mode

- **Arcade:** every ATGM uses second-generation (SACLOS) guidance, so the missile simply flies to wherever your crosshair points. Keep the crosshair on the target until it hits. The fuze is very sensitive, so make sure the flight path doesn't brush scenery on the way, or the missile will burst early.
- **Realistic and Simulator:** each missile uses the guidance it had in real life, so you will meet both first-generation and second-generation systems.

## MCLOS: first generation

With MCLOS you fly the missile yourself using the movement keys:

| Key | Missile goes |
|---|---|
| A | Left |
| D | Right |
| W | Down |
| S | Up |

Because those keys are now steering the missile, your vehicle can't drive while the missile is in flight. Fire from a safe spot. Expect the flight to be twitchy and fairly inaccurate.

## SACLOS: second generation

SACLOS is much easier. The missile follows your crosshair and corrects its course as you move your aim, and your vehicle is free to move once it has fired. Hold the crosshair on the target for the whole flight and it will hit.

You can also take manual control: point your aim at a spot the missile has already flown past, and it will respond to your crosshair movements as you steer it. That lets you guide it on to targets your vehicle can't actually see.

## Kinds of launcher

Nations had different ideas about what a missile vehicle should do, so the designs vary.

### Direct-fire launchers

The most common type: the missile leaves its tube in a straight line. Some vehicles carry them as a backup weapon, like the Warrior; others are dedicated missile carriers, like the RakJPz 2. Performance varies a lot, but these missiles usually bring high launch speed, high penetration, strong damage after penetration, or some mix of these, depending on where the vehicle sits in matchmaking. Dedicated launchers tend to have more range and speed than other vehicles of their era. Some also mount the sight and launcher above the hull, so they can fight from behind a crest with almost nothing showing.

### Upward-launched missiles

Before third-generation missiles, the operator had to stay in view of the target for the whole flight, which left the vehicle exposed. One answer was to launch the missile upwards so it clears the cover in front of the vehicle before levelling off. The Strv 81 (Rb.52) and the Striker work this way.

If the target is close enough that the missile is still climbing, you can use that arc to come down on the thin roof armour, and even reach enemies hiding behind cover. Later missiles of this kind are more manoeuvrable, build up to a reasonable speed over time, and carry tandem warheads to get past ERA.

### Gun-launched missiles

Some tanks fire missiles straight out of the main gun and guide them through the gunner's sight. The M551 was one of the first. Missiles of this kind are usually the quickest around, and you can fire them on the move at any speed. That makes them a match for dedicated launchers, with one trade-off: a longer reload.

In the game they mostly appear on US and USSR tanks at BR 9.0 and above. Their main jobs are dealing with helicopters and other close air support, and hitting distant targets where APFSDS would take too long to arrive. The XM-803 and T-80B, for example, use them against helicopters and lighter vehicles at long range.

## The three generations

| Generation | Guidance | What the operator does | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | MCLOS (manual command to line of sight) | Steers the missile with a controller while watching it; hard to be precise and exposed throughout | SS.11, Malkara, 3M6 Shmel |
| Second | SACLOS (semi-automatic command to line of sight) | Keeps the sight on the target; corrections go to the missile by wire or radio. Some use a laser sight or a camera in the warhead instead. Still has to stay put and exposed | 9M14 Malyutka, Swingfire, AGM-114 Hellfire, BGM-71 TOW |
| Third | Fire-and-forget, with a laser or IR seeker | Free to move as soon as the missile is away | FGM-148 Javelin |

## Missiles and armour

Cheap missiles with HEAT warheads nearly made heavy tanks pointless, and armour changed in response. Composite armour, which mixes metal, plastics and ceramics, stands up to HEAT far better than plain steel. Explosive reactive armour (ERA) uses its own explosive charge to break up a HEAT jet, and later types also interfere with other penetrators.

Missile makers answered with tandem warheads: a small leading charge sets off the ERA, then the main charge hits the armour underneath. Ways to defeat the guidance followed, including jammers, smoke screens and [active protection systems](/guide/mechanics/active-protection-systems/). See [Armour](/guide/mechanics/armour/) for how composite armour and ERA are modelled.
