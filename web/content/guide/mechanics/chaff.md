Chaff is a burst of tiny radar-reflecting strips thrown out of an aircraft's countermeasure dispenser in huge numbers. The strips are made of aluminium, metallised paper, fibreglass or metallised plastic film, and the cloud they form shows up on radar as a swarm of false targets. In the Second World War the British called it "Window".

## Chaff in the game

Chaff arrived in Update 2.5 "Ixwa Strike" and is available on almost every aircraft that has a countermeasure dispenser. It does for radar what [flares](/guide/mechanics/flares/) do for heat seekers: it creates fake signatures that can pull radar-guided missiles off course, set off their proximity fuzes, or make an enemy radar lose its lock on you. How well it works depends on the kind of radar you are up against.

## Against pulse radars

Pulse radars and the missiles that rely on them are the easy case: a chaff burst alone will often shake their lock. A few aircraft have pulse radars with chaff rejection, but you can beat those too by cutting your closure rate towards them.

## Against pulse-Doppler radars: notching

Pulse-Doppler radars measure how fast a target is closing on them. A chaff cloud hangs almost still in the air, so the radar usually recognises it as clutter and ignores it. To get past one you have to make your own closure rate as close to zero as you can.

The way to do that is the **notch**: turn so you are flying at 90 degrees to the radar, either sideways or vertically. Get it right and the radar can no longer tell you apart from the ground or from the chaff you are dropping, so release chaff while you hold the notch.

## Controls

- **Fire countermeasures** releases whatever pattern is selected; by default that is flares and chaff together.
- **Fire chaff** drops chaff only.
- In the **Weapon Selector** menu you can choose what "Fire countermeasures" releases: a mix of both, flares only, or chaff only.

## Where chaff came from

Radar development began in the late 19th century, and by the second half of the 1930s working prototypes of fixed and mobile radar stations were appearing, which made a way to defeat them urgent. Jamming with a separate transmitter works, but it is complicated, risky and expensive. In 1942 the British physicists R.V. Jones and J. Curran proposed a cheaper trick: scatter aluminium strips cut to roughly half the radar's wavelength, and they echo the radar's own signal much like an aircraft does, filling the operator's screen with blips that look real.

It was first used in large numbers in 1943, during the bombing of Hamburg. The radars of German fighters and anti-aircraft guns were swamped, and only 12 of the 791 attacking aircraft were shot down on the first night. The Germans had been developing a similar idea of their own, called "Düppel", and for a time both sides used the technique sparingly, each afraid the other would answer in kind.

Modern chaff is mostly aluminium-coated glass fibre, which is lighter and stays in the air longer. It is now used against airborne radars and radar-homing missiles as well as ground radars. But radars have moved on too: Doppler radars can easily tell a slowly falling cloud from a fast-moving aircraft, so chaff on its own is far less of an obstacle than it was in the 1940s and works best combined with other defences.
