title: Ship Modules
summary: The parts that keep a ship moving, shooting and afloat, what each damage colour means, and which hits can end a ship outright.

Ships run from small boats to cruisers weighing thousands of tonnes, and the more a ship displaces, the harder it is to sink. Sinking is not the only way to win, though. Wreck the right parts and a ship can be left unable to steer, shoot or stay in the fight. Here is what each module does, grouped by whether it affects movement, firepower or survival.

As with tanks, some modules are **linear** (any damage reduces performance, more damage reduces it more) and some are **all-or-nothing** (little effect until destroyed). Damage colours run orange, red, then black for destroyed.

## Movement

### Bridge

The bridge holds the captain and senior crew. It sits in the superstructure, or in the citadel on bigger warships, and may be heavily armoured. Knock it out and nobody is steering until a sailor from one of the compartments steps up. The crew treat that as a low priority, though: they put out fires and deal with urgent damage first, and only then replace the captain.

Larger ships have several officers on the bridge, so it only counts as lost when the module goes black. Note that stopping is not treated as a movement mode of its own: if the bridge goes down while you are slowing to a halt, the ship holds its last order and can keep moving until control returns.

### Steering gear

A damaged rudder can leave you unable to hold a course even with the bridge intact. It repairs itself without any action from you, and a red steering-wheel icon shows at the bottom of the status bar while it is out.

### Engines

Engines drive the ship, and a ship that stops moving is a gift to torpedoes and aircraft. Silencing them completely is not easy, though: many vessels have more than one engine, and even small boats often have two. Engines can catch fire, with the chance depending on the engine type and the projectile. They are linear:

- **Orange:** at least one engine still works.
- **Red:** every engine is out, but the crew's temporary repairs give you minimal movement.
- **Black:** the engines and their crew are destroyed and the ship cannot move.

### Transmission

The transmission carries power from the engines to the propellers. Knock it out and you are as stuck as with dead engines. It does have two things going for it: it never catches fire, and it is usually tucked away where it is hard to hit. It is linear, and its propeller icon changes colour:

- **Orange:** at least one propeller is still more or less working.
- **Red:** every propeller is out, but the crew has patched things up enough for minimum speed.
- **Black:** every propeller is destroyed and the ship temporarily cannot move.

### Funnels

The smoke stacks are part of the exhaust system. A ship can still move if they are damaged or shot away, but its top speed may drop by up to about 15%.

## Firepower

### Turrets and gun mounts

Larger warships carry several turrets, so losing one hurts less. Barrel damage works much like on tanks: slower rate of fire and wider dispersion, even in realistic modes. Heavy damage can jam a turret so it no longer traverses, and a destroyed turret cannot fire. Turret crews are drawn from the compartments and topped up as long as there are sailors to spare.

- **Orange:** at least one turret can fire.
- **Red:** no turret can fire.

### Ammunition hoists

Shells are kept in magazines below and carried up to the turrets by hoists. A broken hoist slows reloading by 10–20%, depending on how badly it is damaged.

### Torpedo tubes

A damaged tube, or a damaged torpedo, cannot be used, and there is a small chance a hit sets one off. They are all-or-nothing:

- **Orange:** at least one tube can still launch.
- **Red:** no tube can launch.

### Depth charges

Depth charges and other anti-submarine weapons are a hazard: if their storage is destroyed, they may explode. Partial damage generally has no effect. The explosion check only happens when the module goes black.

## Survival

### Hull and flooding

A ship floats because of the water it displaces. Holes in the hull let water in and eat into that buoyancy. Bulkheads split the hull into watertight compartments, and as each one floods, buoyancy drops in proportion. Bigger holes, or more of them, flood faster. Because the losses from several compartments can add up to more than 100%, damage control needs to start quickly.

Every ship can repair its hull without researching anything. Dealing with flooding takes two steps: patch the holes, then pump the water out.

### Compartments

Compartments hold the crew who fight fires, patch holes, pump out water, man the guns and more. They burn very easily, and the chance of fire does not depend on how damaged they already are. They are linear: the more damage, the fewer sailors left, and a black compartment means everyone in it is dead. Unlike individual crew modules, compartments are not restocked during the battle. How many sailors each holds varies from ship to ship.

### Magazines

Ammunition lives in magazines, plus ready-use lockers for the turrets. Magazines are all-or-nothing, and a destroyed magazine will normally explode, usually wrecking the ship.

### Fuel tanks

Coal bunkers and liquid fuel tanks are not equally risky. Large internal bunkers rarely blow up, while smaller or external tanks are more likely to catch fire or explode depending on what hits them. They are all-or-nothing: the risk depends mainly on the type of shell, and in rare cases rises once the tank is fully destroyed.

### Pumps

Pumps clear water from flooded compartments. Damaged pumps slow that down but do not affect how fast the hull itself is repaired.

## Command and fire control

### Radio station

The radio room holds the communications gear and the crew who handle outside communication, coordinate spotting and, on some ships, pass on fire-control data. Damage here can weaken or cut long-range spotting, radio-guided links and other features that need communications. On some ships radar or radio direction-finding equipment is tied to this module too, so losing it costs you awareness. Typical effects are poorer spotting reports, weaker fire-control features such as spotting corrections and remote director feeds, and less coordination with aircraft and spotters.

### Fire control rooms and directors

These decide how well the guns can aim as a group. A fire control room holds rangefinders, plotting tables, ballistic computers and the crew who work out firing solutions and send them to the turrets. A fire director is the aiming station, mechanical or electronic, that passes elevation and bearing to the turrets or stabilises the guns.

Damage to either can reduce accuracy, slow down target acquisition and knock out centralised fire control for several turrets at once. Depending on the ship, that may mean more dispersion, losing range and lead corrections, or turrets falling back to local control and aiming independently.
