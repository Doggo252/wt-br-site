Flares are burning decoys that shine brighter than your aircraft in the wavelengths an infrared seeker is watching. If a flare drifts into the missile's field of view, the seeker sees a stronger heat source than you and swaps its attention to it.

## What a flare is

Flares are loaded into countermeasure dispensers, which fire each cartridge individually when they receive an electrical signal. On more modern aircraft the radar warning receiver (RWR) and missile approach warning (MAW) can trigger the dispensers by themselves as soon as they detect a threat.

Each cartridge has two parts: a small ejection charge or gas generator that kicks the flare out of the dispenser, and the flare itself, a larger pyrotechnic charge usually made of magnesium or other metals that burn hotter than your engine exhaust.

## Knowing a missile is coming

Heat-seeking missiles are hard to detect. Only helicopters and modern jet fighters have the equipment to sense them, using either infrared sensors that pick up the missile's rocket motor or radar that picks up the missile itself.

## Timing is everything

Release flares the moment you know a missile is in the air. The closer it gets, the less they help, for two reasons:

- A flare needs a little time after launch to reach full heat.
- At short range your aircraft fills more of the seeker's view than the flares do.

## Flare types

| Type | Burn time | Brightness | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| BOL flare | Very short | Low | Weaker, but you carry many more of them |
| Regular flare | Long | Medium | The middle ground |
| Large calibre flare | Long | Very high | Most effective, but you carry fewer |

## Make yourself a worse target

After you fire flares, cut the afterburner and swing your hot exhaust nozzle away from the missile. Switching off the afterburner sharply lowers your infrared signature, and turning the nozzle away shrinks what the seeker can see of you. Both make the flares the more attractive target.

## When flares stop working

Many modern missiles carry infrared counter-countermeasures (IRCCM) that make flares far less effective. They work in one of two ways:

- **Narrowing the seeker's field of view**, so flares are less likely to appear in it at all.
- **Pausing guidance for a moment** when a flare is seen, so the missile does not chase it.

Against these missiles, flares are only one layer. Helicopters can add [IRCM](/guide/mechanics/ircm/), and radar-guided threats call for [chaff](/guide/mechanics/chaff/) instead.

## A short history

The AIM-9 Sidewinder, introduced in the mid-1950s, proved how deadly infrared guidance could be, and by the end of that decade prototype flares were already being tested. Through the 1960s and 70s heat seekers spread worldwide, and cheap shoulder-launched missiles made slow, low-flying helicopters and transports easy prey, so flares became standard equipment. From the 1970s on, flare chemistry was tuned to burn at temperatures and wavelengths seekers find more attractive, and dispensers learned to fire in timed sequences and at different angles. Flares remain cheap, simple and good against older seekers, but today they sit alongside warning systems and DIRCM as part of a layered defence.
