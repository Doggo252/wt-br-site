Hydropneumatic suspension, also called controlled suspension, lets a vehicle change its ride height and attitude. Each wheel's shock absorber is filled with fluid, which passes the force of a bump on to a gas-filled chamber that acts as the spring. Modern systems control the hydraulics electronically, though the design does not strictly need electronics to work.

## What it does in the game

Every tank that had this suspension in real life has it in War Thunder too, the Type 90 and MBT-70 among them. It gives you two things:

- **More gun elevation and depression.** Tilting the hull adds to what the gun can do on its own. Lowering the front, for example, tips the vehicle forward and buys a few extra degrees of depression, which is exactly what you want when shooting down from a ridge.
- **A little extra height.** Raising the vehicle lets it peek over low obstacles to fire.

Used well, it lets you fit the tank to the terrain instead of hunting for a slope that happens to suit you.

You can't break it: its components are not modelled in the damage model, and battery charge doesn't matter to it either. Its only weak point is the engine. While the engine is knocked out the suspension won't respond, and it comes back once the engine is repaired.

## The three kinds

| Kind | What it can do | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Front-back | Tilt the nose up or down | Strv 103 |
| Left-right | Lean the vehicle to one side or the other | — |
| Up-down | Raise or lower the whole vehicle | — |

The Strv 103, for instance, can rock forwards and backwards but cannot lean sideways.

## Setting up the controls

There are no default keys for the suspension, so you have to assign them yourself. You will find them under Controls → Ground vehicles → Suspension control.

## Background

The oil-and-gas suspension was invented by Paul Magès, who worked for Citroën during the Second World War. It reached the public in the Citroën DS, shown at the Paris Motor Show in 1955, whose ride height stayed the same however heavily it was loaded, could be adjusted by hand, and gave a remarkably smooth ride for its day.

The principle is simple. Each wheel connects to a hydraulic cylinder of oil or hydraulic fluid, which leads to a sealed sphere holding nitrogen gas behind a flexible membrane. When the wheel hits a bump, the fluid is pushed into the sphere and squeezes the gas, and that compression is the spring.

### MBT-70

The joint US–West German MBT-70 programme was one of the first military projects to call for it. Its requirements asked for a "newly designed hydropneumatic suspension" so the gun could depress further than -10°. The tank was never adopted: disagreements between the two countries and a hugely overrun budget got the project shelved.

### Strv 103

The Swedish Strv 103 was the first mass-produced tank with hydropneumatic suspension. Each of its eight road wheels, four per side, had its own gas-hydraulic unit, and the front and rear units were linked so the tank could pitch forwards and backwards. Because the gun was fixed rigidly in the hull with no traverse or elevation drives, the suspension did the job of elevating and depressing it. It could also be locked solid to give the gun a steady platform, which mattered because the tank could not use a conventional stabilised turret.

### Today

Some modern main battle tanks, such as Japan's Type 90 and Type 10, use hydropneumatic suspension for its flexibility. Others, notably the M1 Abrams and Leopard 2, stick with traditional torsion bars, avoiding the extra complexity of fitting and maintaining hydropneumatic units.
