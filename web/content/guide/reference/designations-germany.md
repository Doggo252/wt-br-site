German vehicle names are long because German builds long compound words, and then shortens them into strings of abbreviations like Pz.Kpfw., Sd.Kfz. or FlaRakPz. Once you know the building blocks, almost every name in the German tree can be read at a glance. This page covers the common abbreviations, the wartime aircraft system, tank and Sd.Kfz. numbering, captured vehicles, and the post-war Bundeswehr and naval conventions.

## Reading a weapon name

Most German weapon names follow the pattern **calibre, type, year**. Take the 7,92-mm-MG 34:

- **7,92-mm** is the calibre (the bore diameter), written with a decimal comma. Small arms are given in millimetres; from 2 cm upwards, calibres switch to centimetres.
- **MG** is the type: Maschinengewehr, a machine gun.
- **34** is the year the weapon was introduced, here 1934. On some weapons the number is a model number instead.

## The wartime phonetic alphabet

To keep letters from being misheard over the radio, the wartime German forces spelled them out with code words. The same words occasionally turned up as unofficial shorthand or nicknames for variants, especially on aircraft.

A spelling alphabet existed before the war and still exists in updated forms, but the Nazi regime rewrote it to remove names of Jewish or supposedly non-Germanic origin, and changed some others purely to give them a German spelling. D went from David to Dora, N from Nathan to Nordpol, and Y from Ypsilon to Ypern.

## Luftwaffe aircraft

### The RLM system

Around 1932–33 the Reich Air Ministry (Reichsluftfahrtministerium, RLM) brought in one naming system for all German aircraft, civil and military, replacing the patchwork of names each company had used. Every type got a three-digit number and a two-letter maker code, so "Do" for Dornier or "Bf" for Bayerische Flugzeugwerke. The codes started as a capital and a lower-case letter, though later exceptions such as "BV" (Blohm & Voss) used two capitals.

A few details of the system:

- Inside the RLM's own paperwork, aircraft carried an "8-" prefix (the Bf 109 was 8-109), and gliders "108-".
- Blocks of numbers were often reserved for particular makers, but cancellations, reorganisations and old names led to overlaps and reassignments.
- Captured and foreign-built aircraft got RLM numbers too, normally without a maker code.
- Unofficial names, internal project numbers and even invented designations circulated alongside the official ones. Some came from propaganda, some were Allied mistakes, and a few have stuck in post-war books.

### Why Bf and Me both exist

Bayerische Flugzeugwerke grew out of Udet Flugzeugbau and built aircraft designed by Willy Messerschmitt, who at first worked as an independent designer. Messerschmitt had a poor relationship with State Secretary Erhard Milch, and the RLM gave the company the code "Bf" rather than "Me" for the designer. In 1938 BFW was reorganised as Messerschmitt AG and received "Me". Types designed before the change, including the Bf 108, Bf 109 and Bf 110, kept "Bf" for the rest of the war, which is why the Bf 109 and the Me 262 come from the same company.

Blohm & Voss has a similar history. Its aircraft division started out as Hamburger Flugzeugbau with the code "Ha", but because the parent company was so well known, later designs used "BV", and the RLM eventually made that official.

### Designer codes

Late in the war, Allied bombing and rising demand pushed the RLM to spread production across many factories, often run by different companies. Combined with new designs being rushed through, this led to some aircraft being named after their chief designer instead of the maker:

| Code | Designer | Associated with |
|---|---|---|
| Ta | Kurt Tank | Focke-Wulf |
| Ka | Albert Kalkert | Gothaer |
| Hü | Ulrich Hütter | A university, not a manufacturer |
| Li | Alexander Lippisch | DFS, Messerschmitt |

### Variants and suffixes

- **Letter blocks** mark the main production variants: Ju 88 A, B, C.
- **Numbers** within a block mark smaller changes: Ju 88 A-1, A-4.
- **A new type number** marks a major redesign, usually by going up a hundred: Ju 88 → Ju 188 → Ju 288 → Ju 388 → Ju 488.

Extra suffixes add detail:

| Suffix | German | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| V | Versuchs | Prototype; replaces the variant letter entirely |
| R | Rüstsatz | Ready-made field kit for a particular mission, fitted without factory work |
| U | Umrüst-Bausatz | Conversion kit fitted at the factory or depot, on the production line or during overhaul |
| trop | tropen | Modified for hot, dusty climates such as North Africa, the Mediterranean or southern Russia |

### Nicknames

Some nicknames were official, others came from crews, the public or the Allies:

- **Bf 109 "Messerschmitt"**: the maker's name became shorthand for the aircraft, particularly among the Allies, and it was often just called the "one-oh-nine".
- **Fw 190 "Würger"** (shrike): an official name after a small predatory bird, fitting its aggressive fighter role.
- **Ju 87 "Stuka"**: short for Sturzkampfflugzeug, dive bomber. It caught on so completely that it largely replaced the real designation on both sides.
- **Me 262 "Schwalbe"** (swallow): Germany's first operational jet fighter.
- **He 162 "Volksjäger"** (people's fighter): a late-war emergency fighter built for simple mass production and meant to be flown by quickly trained pilots.

### Engine designations

The RLM also had an internal code for engines. It began with a type prefix, **9** for piston engines and **109** for jets, rockets, turbojets and turboprops, followed by a maker number and a series number. The BMW 801 radial in the Fw 190 was officially **9-801**: 9 for a piston engine, 8 for BMW, 01 for the series.

In practice the system was applied unevenly even within the RLM, and everyone used the maker's name and model instead: BMW 801, DB 605, Jumo 004. The 109-series numbers for jets and rockets didn't strictly follow maker numbering either.

| Maker number | Maker |
|---|---|
| 090–099 | Various smaller makers |
| 1 | BMW (later moved to the 800 block) |
| 2 | Junkers Flugzeug- und Motorenwerke |

| 4 | Argus Motoren |

## Panzerwaffe

Wartime German tank names mixed formal designations, prototype codes and everyday shorthand.

- **Pz.Kpfw. + Roman numeral** is the core: Pz.Kpfw. I up to Pz.Kpfw. VIII, usually followed by a variant such as Ausf. A or Ausf. G.
- **VK (Vollkettenfahrzeug)** marks experimental and pre-production vehicles. The number gives the target weight class and the development sequence, and a letter at the end often names the maker. VK 30.02 (M) is the second prototype in the 30-tonne class, by MAN.
- **Vs.Kfz. (Versuchskraftfahrzeug)** marks other experimental vehicles. These numbers look arbitrary and were often secret, so they were rarely used; companies usually referred to the vehicles by code names or internal numbers instead.
- **Jagdpanzer and Panzerjäger** names for tank destroyers follow the same principles, and many of these vehicles were built on modified or captured chassis.

From around 1940, many tanks also got nicknames, often picked or approved by Hitler, to make them more memorable for propaganda. Panther (Pz.Kpfw. V), Tiger (Pz.Kpfw. VI) and Maus (Pz.Kpfw. VIII) quickly became the names everyone used, crews and Allied reports included, even where they weren't strictly official.

### Sd.Kfz. numbers

Sd.Kfz. (Sonderkraftfahrzeug) was the inventory number given to wartime military vehicles, from unarmoured half-tracks to fully tracked armour. The number range tells you the broad category:

| Range | Category |
|---|---|

| 100–199 | Tanks and vehicles based on them, such as tank destroyers and self-propelled artillery |
| 200–299 | Reconnaissance vehicles, armoured cars, armoured personnel carriers and command tanks |

### Captured vehicles (Beutepanzer)

Germany kept a catalogue of captured foreign equipment, the *Kennblätter fremden Geräts* D 50 series ("data sheets of foreign equipment"). Each volume covered one category, with specifications, identification guides and notes on use: small arms might be in D 50/1, for example, and vehicles were in D 50/12.

Captured tanks and other armour, whether taken in battle or from factories in occupied countries, were called Beutepanzer ("loot tanks"). They were renamed under German rules, usually using a number block for the vehicle type plus a letter in brackets for the country of origin. The names could reflect type, calibre, year or bits of the original name, and with overlapping categories and hurried use at the front, the system wasn't always consistent.

### The E-series

The E-series (Entwicklung, "development") was a 1943–44 plan to simplify German tank production, which had become complicated and expensive. Instead of separate designs like the Panzer IV, Panther and Tiger, it proposed a family of tanks by weight class sharing common parts, with sloped armour and improved suspension, and easier maintenance:

- E-10 and E-25 (light)
- E-50 (medium)
- E-75 (heavy)
- E-100 (super-heavy)

None of them reached mass production before the war ended; only prototypes or unfinished hulls were built.

## Bundeswehr

After the war, the new German army tidied up its abbreviations and moved away a little from the wartime terms. Pz.Kpfw. became KPz (Kampfpanzer), and the various tank destroyer terms were folded into JPz (Jagdpanzer). See the post-war table above for the full set.

Animal names carried on too, and this time they often became part of the official designation, as with the KPz Leopard. Variants are numbered in sequence, much like US tanks: the original Leopard was followed by the Leopard A1, A2 and so on. The name "Leopard 1" was only applied after the Leopard 2 arrived, to tell the two families apart.

## Kriegsmarine

The wartime navy combined traditional class names, project codes and internal designations.

- **Major warships**, meaning battleships (Schlachtschiffe), cruisers (Kreuzer) and destroyers (Zerstörer), were usually named after historical figures, cities or regions.
- **Destroyers and everything smaller**, including U-boats, were also numbered in sequence behind a type prefix such as U, Z or T (see the boats and ships table above).
- **Ship classes** generally used a base class name, with sub-classes lettered A, B and so on. Some carried "(Mob)" for Mobilmachung, "mobilisation", marking simplified versions designed to be built faster in wartime.

| Ship type | Class named after | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Capital ships (CV/BB/BC/CA/CL) | The lead ship | Admiral Hipper class |
| Destroyers and torpedo boats (DD/TB) | Type plus design year | Type 1934 class |
| Submarines (SS) | Type plus Roman numeral | Type VII class |
